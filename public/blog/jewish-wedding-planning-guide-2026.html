Jewish Wedding Planning Guide 2026: Chuppah, Ketubah, Hora & Kosher Venues

חתונה · From Shabbat restrictions to dance floor geometry — what every couple and planner needs to know about Jewish weddings in 2026.

June 23, 2026 · 11 min read · Weddings.io Editorial

Jewish weddings operate on a layer of legal, religious, and logistical requirements that sit completely outside the scope of general wedding planning resources. The Chuppah has structural requirements. The date has legal restrictions. The catering has certification requirements. The Hora has floor space math. A couple who plans a Jewish wedding using generic tools and venues that have never hosted one before will spend the day managing failures that were entirely preventable.

This guide is a working reference — practical, specific, and built around the real operational demands of Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, and Sephardic Jewish weddings in 2026.

Shabbat Date Rules — What the Calendar Prohibits

Jewish law (Halacha) prohibits weddings on Shabbat — from Friday sundown to Saturday night — and on most major Jewish holidays. This is not a guideline. It is a prohibition that your officiating rabbi will enforce. If you book a Saturday 6pm venue before checking the exact Shabbat end time for that date in your city, you may find yourself starting your ceremony inside Shabbat.

Shabbat ends at Havdalah, approximately 45–60 minutes after sunset. The exact time varies significantly by season and latitude:

Most rabbis require the ceremony to begin at least 60 minutes after Havdalah, not immediately after. A Toronto June wedding cannot start until approximately 11:00pm if held on a Saturday. This is why Friday afternoon ceremonies (ending before sundown) or Sunday ceremonies are far more common in summer months for observant families.

Additional prohibited periods: the three weeks before Tisha B'Av (typically mid-July), the counting of the Omer (most of April–May), and the High Holiday period. Check with your rabbi for the specific dates applicable to your tradition.

The Chuppah — Structure, Orientation, and Logistics

The Chuppah (חוּפָּה) is the marriage canopy under which the ceremony takes place. It is not just décor. It is the symbolic home of the new couple. Its structural integrity and placement affect the entire ceremony experience.

Indoor Chuppah Requirements

  • Minimum ceiling height: 12 feet (14+ feet preferred for floral hanging installations)
  • Canopy dimensions: 8×8 feet minimum; 10×10 feet for comfortable movement
  • Pole height: 8–10 feet
  • Weight-bearing capacity: confirm with venue if hanging florals exceed 20 lbs per pole
  • Orientation: face East (toward Jerusalem) if the room layout allows
  • Aisle clearance: 5 feet minimum for processional
  • Space under canopy: Rabbi + couple + 2 witnesses minimum; 6–8 people total is typical

Outdoor Chuppah Requirements

  • Traditional Jewish law prefers outdoor Chuppah — under open sky is the ideal
  • Poles must be secured: minimum 25 lb sandbags per pole, or ground stakes if permitted
  • Wind contingency: have a full indoor backup location confirmed and vendor-briefed by 48 hours prior
  • Sun position: guests should face away from direct sunlight — check the sun's position at your ceremony time before finalising the orientation
  • Nighttime: spotlights aimed at canopy from behind the couple, never into the crowd

The Ketubah — Witness Logistics

The Ketubah (כְּתוּבָּה) is the Jewish marriage contract. It is signed before the ceremony by two witnesses in the presence of the couple and the officiating rabbi. This is a legal document, not a formality — in Orthodox and Conservative communities, the validity of the marriage depends on it being signed correctly.

Witness Requirements by Tradition

TraditionWitness Requirements
OrthodoxTwo Jewish men, unrelated to each other and to both families, Shabbat-observant
ConservativeTwo Jewish adults (men or women), unrelated to each other and to both families
Reform / ReconstructionistTwo adults of any faith, unrelated to both families; women are fully permitted
SephardicFollows Orthodox requirements; specific Sephardic traditions may add a third witness

Practical logistics: identify your two witnesses at least 2 weeks before the wedding. Confirm they will be at the venue at least 45 minutes before the ceremony for the signing. The Ketubah signing typically happens in a private room — not in the ceremony space. The signed document is carried into the ceremony by the rabbi or best man.

The Hora — Dance Floor Mathematics

The Hora is the most physically demanding moment of a Jewish wedding for the venue. Guests form large concentric circles and dance at high intensity. The couple is lifted in chairs. The floor must be clear, the space must be large enough, and the furniture removal must be planned in advance.

⚠️ The Hora cannot be pre-emptively managed. Once it starts, it grows. Guests who were not planning to dance will join. Tables near the dance floor will be pushed back. Brief the venue operations team in advance: the dance floor must be completely clear of tables, chairs, and centrepieces before the Hora begins. This typically means a 10-minute room transition before the bandleader cues the dance.

Kosher Catering — What Venues Must Confirm

Booking a Kosher wedding at a venue that has never done one — or that claims to "accommodate Kosher" without proper certification — is a common and serious mistake. Kosher is not a dietary preference that can be partially accommodated. It is a system of food laws that requires certified supervision.

Eight questions to ask every venue before signing a Kosher contract:

  1. Is a certified Mashgiach (Kosher supervisor) available for the full event including prep?
  2. Are there physically separate preparation areas for meat and dairy?
  3. Are there separate industrial dishwashers or designated separate sinks for meat and dairy?
  4. Does all meat carry a Hechsher (Kosher certification) from a recognised certifying body?
  5. Is all wine Yayin Mevushal (cooked wine, which retains Kosher status when handled by non-Jews)?
  6. Is the venue willing to remove all non-Kosher items from the kitchen during your event?
  7. Has your rabbi reviewed and approved this venue and its certification?
  8. If serving meat at dinner and dairy at dessert, will the 6-hour wait be observed?
